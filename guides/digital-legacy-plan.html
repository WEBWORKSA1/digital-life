Write a digital legacy plan in an hour

A generation ago, a family could find everything that mattered in a filing cabinet. Today it sits behind passwords, and without a plan it can be lost for good.

Reviewed November 2026 · 5 minute read

Why this is urgent, not morbid

A plan helps in far more situations than death: a hospital stay, a stolen phone while travelling, or simply being unreachable when a bill needs paying. Without one, families face locked devices, photos nobody can open, subscriptions that keep charging and accounts that linger for years.

Step 1: Make the inventory (20 minutes)

Use the digital legacy planner to list what exists. Do not include passwords. Cover:

  • Email accounts
  • Banking, investments, payment apps and any cryptocurrency
  • Photo libraries and cloud storage
  • Social and messaging accounts
  • Subscriptions and recurring bills
  • Domains, websites and online businesses
  • Devices and how each is unlocked

Step 2: Decide your wishes (10 minutes)

For each item, choose: give access to a named person, transfer, memorialise, or close and delete. Be specific about photos and messages. Some people want everything preserved; others want private conversations deleted unread. Either is reasonable, but only if someone knows.

Step 3: Use the platforms’ own tools (15 minutes)

Major providers offer settings built for this, usually under account security or privacy:

  • Legacy contact: a person who can request access to your data after your death.
  • Inactive account settings: rules for what happens after a period of no activity, including who is notified.
  • Memorialisation: social networks can freeze a profile as a memorial managed by someone you nominate.

Set these on your main email, phone account and social profiles. They carry more weight with providers than a note asking someone to log in as you, which may breach terms of service.

Step 4: Arrange access to the secrets (10 minutes)

Do not put passwords in your will. A will can become a public document. Reference a separate, private record instead.

Good options:

  • The emergency access feature of your password manager, which lets a trusted person request access after a waiting period you set.
  • A sealed envelope containing your master passphrase and device PIN, kept with your will, a solicitor or in a home safe.
  • For cryptocurrency, clear instructions for the recovery phrase, stored securely and separately. Without it, the assets are unrecoverable.

Step 5: Tell someone (5 minutes)

A plan nobody knows about does not work. Tell your chosen person that the plan exists and where it is kept. They do not need the details today.

Keep it current

Review the inventory once a year and after major changes: a new phone, a new bank, a marriage, a separation or a death in the family. Out-of-date legacy contacts are a common and avoidable problem.

The legal side

Rules on who may access a deceased person’s accounts differ by country and by service. Mention digital assets when you make or update a will, and ask your adviser how local law treats them.
