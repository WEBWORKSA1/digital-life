The 3-2-1 backup, made simple

Phones are lost, laptops fail, accounts get locked and ransomware encrypts files. A backup turns each of those from a disaster into an inconvenience.

Reviewed November 2026 · 5 minute read

The rule

  • 3 copies of anything you cannot replace
  • 2 different kinds of storage
  • 1 copy kept somewhere else

For most households that means: the original on your device, an automatic cloud backup, and a copy on an external drive at home. If the house floods, the cloud survives. If the cloud account is locked or hacked, the drive survives.

Sync is not backup

Sync services mirror changes across devices. Delete a file, or have it encrypted by ransomware, and the change is mirrored too. Many offer a recycle bin or version history for a limited period, which helps, but a real backup keeps older versions independently of your live files.

Phone: ten minutes

  1. Turn on your phone’s built-in cloud backup and check there is enough storage for it to complete.
  2. Turn on photo library backup and confirm recent photos appear when you sign in from another device.
  3. Check that messaging apps with their own backup setting are included.
  4. A few times a year, copy your photos to a computer or external drive as the second kind of storage.

Computer: twenty minutes

  1. Buy an external drive at least twice the size of your data.
  2. Turn on the backup tool built into your operating system and point it at the drive. It will keep hourly or daily versions automatically.
  3. Add an off-site copy: a cloud backup service, or a second drive kept at a relative’s home or workplace and swapped periodically.
  4. Encrypt the backup drive and store its password in your password manager.
Ransomware note: a drive that is always plugged in can be encrypted along with the computer. Unplug the external drive between backups, or rely on a service that keeps protected version history.

What people forget to back up

  • Two-step login backup codes and your password manager recovery kit
  • Photos that exist only in a chat app
  • Documents saved to the desktop or downloads folder
  • Email, if your address comes from an internet provider you might leave
  • Scans of passports, certificates and insurance documents

Test a restore

A backup you have never restored from is a hope, not a backup. Once a year, pick a file, restore it to a different location and open it. Check the date of the most recent backup while you are there; silent failures from full storage are common.

Keep it running

Automation is what makes backups work. Set them to run on their own, respond to storage-full warnings promptly and put an annual restore test in your calendar.
