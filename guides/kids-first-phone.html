A child’s first phone: a setup plan

The best time to set rules and controls is before the phone is handed over. Afterwards, every limit feels like something being taken away.

Reviewed November 2026 · 5 minute read

Is your child ready?

There is no universally right age. Readiness is a better guide than a birthday. Ask whether your child generally follows agreed rules, tells you when something goes wrong, looks after belongings and has a real need, such as travelling independently. If the need is only calls and messages, a basic phone or a smartwatch may do the job for a year or two.

Before you hand it over

  1. Create a child account in your phone platform’s family system instead of using an adult account with a false date of birth. Age-appropriate protections depend on it.
  2. Turn on purchase and download approval so new apps and spending come to you first.
  3. Set content restrictions for apps, web, music and video to match their age.
  4. Set downtime overnight and during school hours, with calls to parents always allowed.
  5. Review privacy settings: location sharing with parents only, contacts limited to people you know, profiles private.
  6. Lock the settings with a parent passcode your child does not know.

Start small and add

Begin with calls, messages, camera, maps and a few agreed apps. Add more as trust builds. It is far easier to grant a new freedom than to withdraw one. Many families delay social media well beyond the first phone; check each platform’s minimum age and use its teen or supervised mode.

Write a family agreement together

A short written agreement, made with your child, works better than rules imposed. Cover:

  • Where the phone sleeps (not in the bedroom)
  • Phone-free times, such as meals and homework
  • Who they may message and what to do about strangers
  • Asking before downloading or buying
  • What you can see as a parent, stated openly
  • What happens if rules are broken, and how freedoms are earned

Sign it yourself as well. Children notice whether adults follow the same rules at dinner.

The conversations that matter most

The one promise to make: “If you see something upsetting or make a mistake online, tell me. You will not lose your phone for telling the truth.” Children who fear confiscation hide problems.
  • Pictures are permanent. Anything sent can be saved and shared.
  • People online may not be who they say. Never share address, school or live location, and never meet an online-only contact alone.
  • Pressure is a warning sign. Anyone demanding photos, secrecy or money should be reported to you immediately. Make clear they will not be in trouble.
  • Kindness counts online too.

Controls are a scaffold, not a substitute

Filters miss things and determined children find workarounds. Controls buy time while judgement develops; your ongoing interest in what they do online is what actually protects them. Review settings together every few months and loosen them as they show responsibility.
