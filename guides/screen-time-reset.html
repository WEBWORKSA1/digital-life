The screen time reset

Many apps are engineered by large teams to hold attention. Relying on willpower alone against that is an unfair contest. Changing how the phone is set up is a fairer one.

Reviewed November 2026 · 5 minute read

1. Measure before you judge

Open your phone’s screen time or digital wellbeing dashboard and look at three numbers for the past week: daily average, pickups per day and the top three apps. Most people are surprised by one of them. Put your daily average into the screen time calculator to see it as days per year.

2. Decide what the phone is for

Write two short lists: what you want your phone to do for you, and what you would rather it did less of. This is your filter for every step below. The goal is a phone that serves the first list, not a number to feel guilty about.

3. Cut notifications to people and essentials

Every notification is someone else deciding what you look at next. Go through your notification settings app by app:

  • Keep: calls, messages from real people, calendar, banking alerts, deliveries on the day.
  • Switch off: likes, suggestions, promotions, news alerts, games and anything that says “you might be interested”.
  • Summarise: use scheduled notification summaries for the rest.

4. Redesign the home screen

  • First screen: tools only. Maps, camera, calendar, notes, phone.
  • Move social, video and news apps into a folder on the last screen, or remove them and use the browser version.
  • Sign out of the most distracting app. The small friction of logging in breaks the automatic habit.
  • Try greyscale mode in the evening; a colourless feed is far less compelling.

5. Protect sleep

Set a sleep schedule or bedtime mode that silences the phone and dims the screen an hour before bed. Charge it outside the bedroom and use a basic alarm clock. This single change removes both the last scroll of the night and the first of the morning.

Replace, do not just remove. A habit needs somewhere to go. Decide in advance what you will do in the moments you usually reach for the phone: a book by the sofa, a short walk, a text to an actual friend.

6. Use limits as a prompt, not a punishment

App timers work best when they ask a question rather than build a wall. When the limit appears, pause and decide whether you want more time. Often the answer is no, and that moment of choice is the whole point.

7. Review after two weeks

Check the dashboard again. Keep what worked, drop what did not and adjust one thing. Screen habits shift with life, so repeat the review every few months.

When it is more than a habit

If screen use is affecting sleep, work, relationships or mood and you cannot change it alone, talk to a doctor or mental health professional. That is a health conversation, not a failure of discipline.
