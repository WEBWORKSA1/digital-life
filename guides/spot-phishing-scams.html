Five signals that expose a scam message

Scams change their costume every month: a parcel, a toll, a bank, a boss, a grandchild. The mechanics underneath barely change at all.

Reviewed November 2026 · 5 minute read

1. It arrives unexpectedly

You did not ask for it. A delivery you are not waiting for, a refund you did not claim, a security alert for something you did not do. Unexpected contact is not proof of fraud, but it is where every check should begin.

2. It creates urgency or fear

Your account will be closed today. A warrant has been issued. The offer ends in ten minutes. Pressure exists to stop you thinking and to stop you asking someone else. Genuine organisations give you time and several ways to respond.

3. It asks for a secret or a payment

Passwords, one-time codes, card numbers, identity numbers, or money sent by gift card, cryptocurrency, wire or payment app. These payment methods are favoured because they are difficult or impossible to reverse.

4. It gives you the route to respond

A link to tap, a number to call, an attachment to open, an app to install. The scammer needs you to stay inside a channel they control. Look closely at links: the true destination is the part immediately before the first single slash, and look-alike names with extra words or hyphens are a favourite trick.

5. It asks for secrecy or bypasses normal process

Do not tell the bank staff what the transfer is for. Do not mention this to your manager. Buy the cards at a different shop. Any instruction to hide what you are doing is the clearest signal of all.

The habit that beats all five: never act through the message. Close it, then contact the organisation yourself using its official app, the number printed on your card or a web address you type. If it is real, it will still be there in ten minutes.

AI has raised the quality, not changed the rules

Spelling mistakes are no longer a reliable clue. Messages can be fluent and personalised, voices can be cloned from a short clip and video calls can be faked. Families and teams can protect themselves with a simple agreed safe word for any urgent request involving money, and a rule that such requests are always confirmed by calling back on a known number.

If you already clicked or paid

  1. Contact your bank or card provider immediately; speed decides whether money can be recovered.
  2. Change the password of any account you entered details for, and anywhere it was reused.
  3. If you installed something, disconnect the device from the internet and have it checked.
  4. Report it to your national fraud or consumer protection agency. See our resources.

There is no shame in it. These operations are professional and they catch experts too.
