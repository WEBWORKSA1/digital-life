Two-step login and passkeys, explained

A password alone is a single lock. Two-step login adds a second one that a thief on the other side of the world does not have.

Reviewed November 2026 · 5 minute read

The idea in one sentence

Two-step login, also called two-factor or multifactor authentication, asks for something you know (the password) and something you have (your phone or a key). A leaked password is no longer enough to get in.

The methods, from good to best

MethodHow it worksVerdict
Text message codeA code arrives by SMSMuch better than nothing; weak against SIM swap fraud
Authenticator appAn app shows a code that changes every 30 secondsStrong and free; the sensible default
Push approvalYou tap approve on your phoneStrong, if you never approve a prompt you did not trigger
Security keyA small hardware key you plug in or tapStrongest; resists phishing
PasskeyYour device proves who you are after a fingerprint, face or PINReplaces the password and resists phishing

Why passkeys are different

A passkey is a pair of cryptographic keys. The private half never leaves your device or password manager; the website holds only the public half. There is nothing to type, so there is nothing to trick you into typing on a fake site. Where a service offers a passkey, add one and keep your existing two-step method as a fallback.

The five accounts to protect first

  1. Email. It can reset every other account.
  2. Banking and payments.
  3. Your phone and cloud account, which holds photos, backups and device location.
  4. Your password manager.
  5. Your main social profile, which scammers use to target your contacts.

Look in each service’s security settings for “two-step verification”, “two-factor authentication” or “passkeys”.

Do not skip the backup codes

When you switch it on, the service offers one-time backup codes. Save them offline, on paper or in your password manager’s secure notes. They are how you get back in if your phone is lost. Also add a second method where possible, such as a spare security key.

Golden rule: a real organisation will never call, text or email asking you to read out a code. Anyone who asks for one is trying to log in as you.

Moving to a new phone

Before wiping the old phone, transfer your authenticator app using its export or cloud backup feature and confirm the codes work on the new device. Losing the authenticator without backup codes is the most common way people lock themselves out.
