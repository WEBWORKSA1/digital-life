Your data was in a breach. Now what?

Almost everyone with an email address has been in at least one breach. The damage depends far less on the breach than on what you do afterwards.

Reviewed November 2026 · 5 minute read

First, check it is genuine

Fake breach notices are themselves a phishing tactic. Do not tap links in the message. Go to the company’s website directly, look for an official statement, or check a breach notification service. Our breach radar lists newly disclosed incidents.

The first hour

  1. Change the password for the affected account to a new unique one.
  2. Change it anywhere you reused it. Attackers test leaked email and password pairs on banks, shops and email providers automatically. This is called credential stuffing.
  3. Secure your email. New password, two-step login on, and check for forwarding rules or recovery addresses you did not add.

The first day

  • Turn on two-step login for the breached account and sign out of all other sessions.
  • If payment details were involved, call your bank, replace the card and switch on transaction alerts.
  • If government ID numbers, tax numbers or dates of birth were exposed, place a fraud alert or credit freeze with the credit bureaus in your country. A freeze stops new credit being opened in your name and can be lifted when you need it.

The first week and beyond

  • Review statements for unfamiliar charges, including small test payments.
  • Take up free credit or identity monitoring if the company offers it, enrolling only through its official site.
  • Stay sceptical for months. Criminals use breached details, such as your address or the last digits of a card, to make later calls and messages sound legitimate.
Why old breaches still matter: data is traded and re-packaged for years. If a password from a 2019 breach is still in use anywhere, it is still a live risk today.

Reduce the impact of the next one

You cannot prevent companies from being breached. You can make each breach nearly harmless:

  • A password manager so no password is ever reused.
  • Two-step login on every important account.
  • Give services only the data they need. Skip optional fields and delete accounts you no longer use.
  • Use email aliases for sign-ups where your provider supports them, so a leaked address can be switched off.
